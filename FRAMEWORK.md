# The PuhuScribe Framework

**A research-grounded model for acquiring a language — not just studying one.**

by Fumnanya · for PuhuScribe

---

## Why this document exists

PuhuScribe is not "another flashcard app." It is built on an explicit theory of how
adults actually come to speak a new language. This document is the canonical statement
of that theory — the intellectual ground the whole product stands on.

Most of that ground was laid by **Stephen D. Krashen**, whose *Monitor Theory* of second
language acquisition is one of the most influential — and most tested — models in applied
linguistics. We stand on his shoulders, take what four decades of later research has
confirmed and corrected, and turn it into something a learner can actually walk through.

Primary source for this document:

> Krashen, S. D. (1981). *Second Language Acquisition and Second Language Learning.*
> Pergamon Press. (First internet edition, 2002.)

Page citations throughout (e.g. *p.3*) refer to that edition. Where we lean on later
research that revises Krashen, it is named explicitly so the framework cannot be
ambushed by "but Krashen also said…".

## Part 1 — The science we are standing on

Krashen's model is built on one core split and five hypotheses. You need all of them to
understand why PuhuScribe is shaped the way it is.

### 1.1 The core split: Acquisition vs. Learning

Krashen argues adults have **two completely separate systems** for developing a second
language (p.1):

- **Acquisition** — *subconscious.* The same process a child uses for their first
  language. It happens through "meaningful interaction in the target language — natural
  communication — in which speakers are concerned not with the form of their utterances
  but with the messages they are conveying and understanding" (p.1). You are not aware it
  is happening. You end up with a *feel* for what is correct.
- **Learning** — *conscious.* "Knowing about" the language: rules, terminology, error
  correction. This is what classrooms have traditionally optimised for (p.2).

**The bombshell — the "non-interface" claim:** Krashen holds that learning does **not**
turn into acquisition. Consciously memorising a rule does not, by itself, ever become the
effortless ability to use it in speech. They are different systems that develop in
different ways (p.83, the *Routines and Patterns* chapter — memorised language is shown to
be a *third* category that does not convert into either system).

> This single claim is why a grammar-first app can leave a learner fluent in *rules* and
> still unable to *talk*. It is the hinge the entire PuhuScribe design turns on.

*Honest caveat, stated up front:* the strict non-interface position is the most contested
part of Krashen's theory. Later **skill-acquisition theory** (DeKeyser, 2007 and after)
shows that deliberate practice *can* "proceduralise" conscious knowledge into something
fast and automatic. PuhuScribe takes the **hybrid** position — see Part 4. We do not claim
learning is useless; we claim it is not the *engine*.

### 1.2 The Monitor

If learned rules don't power speech, what are they for? Krashen's answer: conscious
learning has exactly one job — it acts as a **Monitor**, an *editor* that inspects and
corrects output (p.2).

But the Monitor can only run when **all three conditions** are met at once (pp.2–3):

1. **Time** — enough of it to stop and think. Normal conversation rarely gives you this.
2. **Focus on form** — you must be attending to *correctness*, not just to the message.
3. **Knowing the rule** — you must actually hold a correct mental version of the rule.

Krashen's dry observation: the one situation where all three reliably hold is "a grammar
test!" (p.3). In real speech they almost never do. So the Monitor is useful for **writing
and prepared speech**, and close to useless mid-conversation.

**Three kinds of Monitor user** (Chapter 1, summarised p.18):

| Type | Behaviour | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Overuser** | Checks rules constantly; won't speak until sure | Hesitant, blocked, often silent |
| **Underuser** | Ignores rules; goes entirely on feel | Fluent but error-prone |
| **Optimal user** | Uses rules to edit *when it doesn't block communication* | Fluent *and* accurate — the goal |

### 1.3 The founding story — "S, the Finnish speaker"

The reason this matters for *this* product is a single case study.

Krashen's textbook example of the **overuser** — the person the rules-first path breaks —
is a participant labelled **"S"** (researchers anonymise subjects by initials; "S" is a
label, not a name). S was a **native Finnish speaker learning English**, studied by
Stafford and Covitt (1978). She knew a great deal of English grammar. Her *written*
English was accurate. And yet (p.15):

> *"She speaks very little, because she tries to remember and use grammar rules before
> speaking."* — and in her own words: *"I feel bad… when I put words together and I don't
> know nothing about the grammar."*

S had done the studying. She had the rules. The rules became a cage.

**The honest mapping:** S was learning English *from* Finnish — the mirror image of
PuhuScribe's first learner, who is learning Finnish *from* English. The *direction* is
reversed. But the *failure mode* is identical, and it is the exact weakness named in our
own learner profile: **"forming spontaneous sentences — mind goes blank"** and
**"speaking confidence."** That is not a vocabulary gap. In Krashen's terms it is **Monitor
overuse driven by a high Affective Filter** (see 1.5). PuhuScribe exists, in large part, to
keep its learners from becoming S.

### 1.4 The Natural Order, and the Input Hypothesis

- **Natural Order** (Chapter 4): grammatical structures are acquired in a fairly fixed
  order that is *not* the order they're usually taught, and that order resists being
  forced by instruction (p.51). Implication: you cannot simply "teach" a structure into
  someone's spontaneous speech before they're ready for it.
- **Input Hypothesis** (Chapters 8–9): we acquire language in **one** way — by
  understanding messages that are **a little beyond** our current level. Krashen calls this
  **comprehensible input**, or **i+1** (where *i* is your current level). Not output, not
  drills — *understanding* slightly-stretching input (pp.9–10). His closing line:

> *"We teach language best when we use it for what it was designed for: communication."*
> (p.10)

### 1.5 The Affective Filter

Even perfect comprehensible input can bounce off. Krashen, building on Dulay and Burt,
describes an **Affective Filter** — an emotional gate (pp.21–22). When anxiety is high,
confidence low, or fear of mistakes strong, the filter is **up** and input is blocked.
When the learner is relaxed, motivated, and unafraid, the filter is **down** and input
gets through to the acquisition system.

This is why two learners with identical input get different results — and why "just
study harder" can make speaking *worse*, by raising anxiety.

### 1.6 Routines and Patterns (the chunk insight)

Krashen and Scarcella (Chapter 7) show that **memorised whole phrases** ("How are you?")
and **patterns with slots** ("That's a ___") are a *distinct* category — usable before you
have any grammar at all, and valuable for managing real conversation early (p.83). This is
the scientific licence for teaching survival chunks on day one, before a single rule.

## Part 2 — The framework: a staircase on two rails

PuhuScribe turns the science above into something a learner can walk through. The model
has two parts: the **staircase** (the four visible stages a learner moves through) and the
**two rails** (the engine that runs underneath every stage and actually produces
acquisition).

```
   THE STAIRCASE  (what the learner consciously does)
                                              ┌──────────┐
                                              │   FLOW   │  ← Acquisition
                                  ┌──────────┐│          │
                                  │   REPS   ││          │
                      ┌──────────┐│          ││          │
                      │  RULES   ││          ││          │
          ┌──────────┐│          ││          ││          │
          │  WORDS   ││          ││          ││          │
          └──────────┘└──────────┘└──────────┘└──────────┘
   ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
   RAIL 1 — COMPREHENSIBLE INPUT (i+1)      ← the fuel
   ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
   RAIL 2 — LOW AFFECTIVE FILTER            ← the valve
   ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
       LEARNING  ───────────────────────────►  ACQUISITION
```

The staircase is what the learner *does*. The rails are what make it *stick*. **Climbing
the stairs without the rails is exactly how you produce an "S"** — someone at the top of
the grammar staircase who still cannot speak.

### Stage 1 — WORDS

**What the learner does:** builds raw material — individual words *and* whole-sentence
chunks (*"Paljonko se maksaa?"*, *"hauska tutustua"*, *"en tiedä"*).

**Its real job (Krashen):** this is the **Routines and Patterns** strand (1.6). Survival
chunks let the learner communicate *before* they know any grammar — which starts the
acquisition engine immediately and builds early confidence (filter down).

### Stage 2 — RULES

**What the learner does:** learns the grammar system through PuhuScribe's signature
teaching method — **Origin → Logic → Rule → Exceptions → Examples**. Never a rule without
its *why*; the exception shown immediately; every example from real daily life.

**Its real job (Krashen):** this builds the **Monitor** (1.2) — an *editor* for moments
when the learner has time and focus (writing, prepared speech, self-checking). **Crucially,
RULES is not a gate the learner must pass before being allowed to speak.** Treating it as a
gate is precisely what created "S." Grammar here serves conversation; it does not police
entry to it.

### Stage 3 — REPS

**What the learner does:** controlled practice — fill-in-the-blank, transformation,
translation, negation — plus error tracking that re-surfaces recurring mistakes.

**Its real job:** sharpen the Monitor, and provide a little **pushed output** (Swain,
1985 — producing language forces deeper processing than just receiving it). This is where
PuhuScribe departs from strict Krashen and sides with skill-acquisition theory: practice
helps *proceduralise* knowledge (see Part 4). **But accuracy is not fluency.** REPS is a
bridge, not the destination — and a learner who lives here becomes an overuser.

### Stage 4 — FLOW

**What the learner does:** spontaneous, real use — live conversation in the target
language, grounded in their actual life (for our first learner: the gym, photography
clients, the tram, the evening cleaning job).

**Its real job (Krashen):** this is **Acquisition itself** (1.1) — the *only* stage that
builds spontaneous speech, and it works **only because** it delivers comprehensible input
(Rail 1) inside a low-anxiety space (Rail 2). The aim is the **optimal Monitor user**:
fluent first, accurate when there's room to be.

## Part 3 — The two rails (the actual engine)

The stairs get the attention. The rails do the work. They are **always on, under every
stage** — WORDS, RULES, REPS, and FLOW are each delivered *on* these rails or they don't
produce acquisition.

### Rail 1 — Comprehensible Input (i+1)

Every word, rule, drill, and conversation is pitched **just above** the learner's current
level but still understandable (1.4). This is the fuel of acquisition. Concretely it means:

- the AI tutor converses *in* the target language at a level the learner can follow,
  stretching slightly, rather than defaulting to English explanation;
- examples and conversations are scaffolded so meaning is always graspable from context;
- difficulty rises with the learner, never in a cliff.

### Rail 2 — Low Affective Filter

Everything is engineered to keep the emotional gate **down** (1.5), because input only
gets in when anxiety is low:

- **speak before you're "ready"** — the product invites output early and treats errors as
  data, not failure;
- **direct but warm feedback, zero shame** — correct clearly, explain why immediately, no
  empty praise and no scolding;
- **grounded in the learner's real life** — relevance lowers threat and raises motivation
  (Krashen's *integrative/instrumental motivation*, pp.21–22);
- **rules demoted from gatekeeper to editor** — removing "you may not speak until it's
  correct" is the single biggest filter-lowering move, and the direct antidote to the
  "S" trap.

## Part 4 — Our position on the Krashen debate (so we can't be ambushed)

Krashen is foundational but not unchallenged. An informed critic will raise two points; here
is exactly where PuhuScribe stands.

**Objection 1 — "Krashen says explicit grammar and drills barely help. So why do you teach
rules at all?"**
We do not hold the strict non-interface position. We side with **skill-acquisition theory**
(DeKeyser, 2007): deliberate practice *can* turn conscious knowledge into fast, automatic
use — it *proceduralises*. So RULES and REPS earn their place as **scaffolding and an
accelerator**, not as the engine. Our stance in one line:

> **Input + low filter is the engine (Krashen). Explicit rules + drills are scaffolding —
> a Monitor and an accelerator — that we deliberately fade as acquisition takes over.**

**Objection 2 — "Comprehensible input alone is enough; output doesn't drive acquisition."**
Strict Krashen says input is sufficient. We add a measured role for **output** (Swain's
*Output Hypothesis*, 1985): producing language — being "pushed" to say things — forces
processing that pure listening doesn't. That is the defensible reason REPS and the speaking
stages exist, without contradicting the primacy of input.

This **hybrid** is both more current than pure Krashen and more rigorous than the
grammar-grind apps. It is the strongest ground available to stand on.

---

## Part 5 — One-line summary

> **PuhuScribe walks a learner from *learning* to *acquisition* up a four-step staircase —
> WORDS · RULES · REPS · FLOW — riding on two rails: comprehensible input and a low
> affective filter. Rules exist to serve conversation, not to gate it. The goal is not a
> learner who knows the grammar. It is a learner who forgets they ever needed it.**
